How to Use Your Water Meter to Find a Hidden Leak

To find a hidden leak with your water meter, turn off every tap and appliance, read the meter, wait two hours with no water used, and read it again. If the reading changed, you probably have a leak.

A second step, closing the house shutoff and watching the meter once more, tells you whether the leak is inside the house or in the buried line.

The test in plain terms

The EPA's advice is simple: read the meter, use no water for two hours, then read it again. Leaks hide because water from a toilet flapper, a buried line or a slab does not show up on the floor. The meter does not care where the water goes, only that it is moving.

The stakes are real. The EPA says the average household's leaks can waste more than 9,300 gallons of water every year. A small continuous leak is quiet, and the first sign is often the bill.

Run the test in seven steps

Pick a time when nobody will be home or asleep. An evening or a night works, so water use is easy to avoid.

  1. Shut off everything that uses waterClose taps, stop the dishwasher and washing machine, and switch off any ice maker or humidifier. Check that no one flushes during the test.
  2. Find the meterIt usually sits under a lid in the ground near the street or property line. Lift the lid carefully and look for spiders or debris before reaching in.
  3. Look at the small dialUC Master Gardeners say the smaller blue dial moves when water is flowing. With all water off, it should hold still. If it is turning, you can already suspect a leak.
  4. Write down the full readingNote every digit, or take a photo. You will compare it to the later reading.
  5. Wait two hours without using any waterDo not flush the toilet, run a tap or let the sprinklers start. Even one flush ruins the test.
  6. Read it againIf the number has gone up, water is leaving the system somewhere. If it is identical, you probably do not have a continuous leak.
  7. Narrow it with the house shutoffClose the main shutoff valve at the house, wait a few minutes, then watch the meter dial. We cover what the result means below.

What your meter result means

Two readings, taken together, point to where the leak is.

Meter test resultWhere the leak likely isWho to call
Meter moves with all water off, and stops when the house shutoff is closedInside the house: a fixture, pipe or slabLeak detection plumber
Meter keeps moving even with the house shutoff closedBuried line between the meter and the house, or the shutoff itselfService line repair
Meter does not move, but the bill is highHeavy use, irrigation, or a leak that only runs at certain timesCheck bills and sprinkler timers
Meter moves only slightly, then stopsA toilet flapper or a slow dripTest the toilets first

If the meter does not move during your test, a leak can still exist, since some leaks only run when a pump or timer starts. A plumber can test the system under pressure and listen to the pipes.

Rule out the easy leaks first

The EPA lists worn toilet flappers, dripping faucets and other leaking valves as the most common sources of household leaks. Toilets are the first suspect. Drop a little food coloring in the tank and wait. If color reaches the bowl without flushing, the flapper is leaking, and a toilet repair is usually quick.

Check faucets and hose bibs for drips, and walk the yard if you have sprinklers. A single stuck irrigation valve can run for days without anyone noticing. A dripping tap is a small fix, and our faucet repair page covers what that visit involves.

When the meter moves and nothing inside is running

If you closed the house shutoff and the meter kept turning, the water is escaping before it reaches your home. That points to the service line that carries water from the meter to the house, or to the shutoff valve itself.

If the meter stopped when the shutoff closed, the leak is inside your plumbing. A warm spot on the floor, a musty smell, or a hiss from a wall may narrow it down. On a slab foundation, a hidden pipe leak can also run under the concrete, and our slab leak repair page explains how those are located.

Do not dig to chase a buried line. Call 811 first so utilities are marked, and have a licensed plumber confirm the location before any digging begins.

Wasco's water rules and the meter

The City of Wasco's water conservation chapter requires leaks to be fixed, and it treats 48 hours after discovery as a reasonable time to do so. A meter that proves you have a leak therefore starts a clock, so get the repair booked soon rather than letting it run.

The city's water comes from groundwater wells, with about 94 miles of distribution lines according to its 2022 water quality report. If you suspect a problem on the utility side, such as damage at the meter box itself, tell the City of Wasco's water utility rather than digging on your own.

Keep your two meter readings and the time between them. They are useful when you talk to the utility or the plumber.

Getting help after the test

Once the test shows a leak, stop guessing and have it located. A plumber can use acoustic tools that listen for leak noise, thermal tools that show temperature differences, and pressure tests to pinpoint the spot without tearing out a wall.
