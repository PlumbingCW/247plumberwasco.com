What to Set Your Tankless Water Heater To

For most Wasco households, 120 degrees Fahrenheit is the right place to start. Raise it only if you have a clear reason, and lower it if anyone in the house is young, elderly or sensitive to heat.

By the Wasco 24/7 Plumbing team. Published October 29, 2026. Updated October 29, 2026.

Call (661) 338-1180 now

Start at 120 degrees and adjust from there

The U.S. Department of Energy says most households only need the water heater set at 120 degrees Fahrenheit. That applies to tankless units as well as tanks. At that setting the water is hot enough for showers, sinks and many dishwashers, and it is easier on the unit than a higher target.

Tankless heaters heat water as it flows, so the temperature you set is the temperature the unit aims to deliver at the tap. If the shower still feels too cool, the cause is often flow, distance or a limited unit, not the setpoint. The sections below show how to tell.

Matching the setting to your situation

Use this as a decision guide before you touch the dial or the remote controller.

How to change the setting without guessing

Controls differ between brands, so follow the manual for your model. The sequence below applies to most units.

  1. Find the controllerMany units have a wall-mounted remote near the heater or in a hallway. Some keep the control on the unit itself. If neither shows a temperature display, the setting may be fixed by a dip switch or by the installer.
  2. Note the current settingWrite it down. Then run a hot tap for a minute and feel the water at the sink with your hand under a stream, not directly on the faucet.
  3. Change by one stepMove the setpoint a single step and test again after a minute of flow. Small changes make it easier to see what actually helped.
  4. Test at the farthest fixtureThe tap farthest from the unit tells you more than the one beside it. If only that tap is cool, the issue is travel time in the pipe.
  5. Look for error codesIf the display shows a code or the unit shuts down, stop adjusting. Codes usually point to flow, venting, gas supply or scale, and each of those needs a technician.

Why a hotter setpoint is not a free upgrade

A higher target means the unit works harder on every call for hot water. It also raises the scald risk, because water at the tap arrives hotter than many people expect. Most households do better with 120 and a mixing valve on the shower than with a high setting and constant tap adjustments.

Heat also tends to speed up mineral scale inside the heat exchanger. Scale is the most common reason a tankless heater loses output over time, and it is the reason tankless water heater service often includes a flush. The DOE says tankless units last about 20 years, and keeping scale under control is a large part of getting there.

Signs the problem is not the temperature setting

People often raise the setting and still get cool water. These are the usual culprits.

  • ✓A short burst of cold between two hot flows. This happens when the tap is closed and reopened quickly. The heater shuts off between uses, and a slug of cold water passes through before it fires again.
  • ✓Hot water that fades when two fixtures run. Every tankless unit has a flow limit. Running a shower and a dishwasher together can exceed what it can heat at the target temperature.
  • ✓Hot water that slowly gets worse over months. Scale in the heat exchanger or a clogged inlet filter reduces output. The unit may need a descaling flush.
  • ✓Sudden loss of heat with no code. Check that the gas supply, power and vent are intact, and call a plumber if anything looks wrong. Gas-fired units burn fuel, so leave the area and call SoCalGas or 911 if you smell gas.

Wasco note: test your water before you chase a hotter setting

The City of Wasco's 2022 water quality report lists groundwater from six active wells but does not report hardness. Nearby towns publish different numbers, so you cannot borrow a neighbor's result without checking.

A hardness test strip or a lab test at your own tap tells you how aggressively scale may build in a tankless unit. If hardness turns out to be high, a water softener can protect the heat exchanger, and the right flush schedule matters more than the setpoint.

When the setting is right and the water is still wrong

A properly set tankless unit that still delivers lukewarm water needs a technician to look at flow, gas pressure, venting or scale. According to CSLB, the C-36 plumbing classification covers installing equipment that heats water, so check the technician's license at cslb.ca.gov. The reported water around Wasco varies. Shafter's recent city reports show average hardness of 92 ppm in 2025, with individual wells ranging from 17 to 207 ppm. McFarland's 2025 city report shows soft water at 11 ppm. Those numbers show why two nearby homes can have very different scale problems, and why testing at your own tap is more reliable than guessing.

Cold January nights also matter if the unit sits in a garage or on an outside wall. Bakersfield, the nearest NWS climate station, has an average January low of 40.5 degrees Fahrenheit, and freezing nights are possible. Check what your manual says about freeze protection before winter.
