A patch of lawn that stays soggy, pressure that fades at every tap, or a meter that turns with the house shut off can all point to the buried line between the meter and your home.

That pipe is the water service line, and it is a different job from indoor repiping or from simply locating a leak.

When the buried supply line fails, water can surface in the yard, flood a garage floor or silently drain away while the meter keeps counting. Line leaking: shut it off and keep clear

A leaking buried line can undermine soil and make walking areas unsafe. These steps keep it contained.

Before the plumber arrives

  • !Close your main valve. It is usually at the house or near the meter. Turn it fully clockwise.
  • !Check the meter. If the dial still moves with your valve closed, the leak sits between the meter and the valve, and the plumber needs to know.
  • !Stay off soft ground. Saturated soil can give way, especially near a driveway edge or a slab.
  • !Do not dig yet. Gas, electric and other lines are buried in yards. Call 811 before any digging, and the plumber will follow the same rule.
  • !Smell for gas. If you smell gas near the work area, leave on foot and call SoCalGas or 911 from a safe distance before you call us.

Where the service line runs and who handles which part

The City of Wasco's 2022 water quality report describes a groundwater system with six active wells and about 94 miles of distribution lines. Your service line is the short branch that taps one of those mains and passes through a meter on its way to your home. The city's water department can tell you where its responsibility ends, so ask before assuming.

Plumbing contractors in California work from the property owner's side of the meter to the building, according to the state license classification for plumbers. That is the section a licensed plumber repairs or replaces on this job. Anything on the city's side of the meter is for the water utility.

Signs the line itself is leaking

  • ✓Wet ground with no sprinkler on. A damp strip or a sinking patch between the meter and the house, even in a dry week.
  • ✓A meter that moves with the house off. Run the two-hour test. A change in the reading means water is leaving somewhere downstream of the meter.
  • ✓Pressure that fades over time. A line corroding from the inside, or leaking, delivers less at every fixture.
  • ✓Bills that creep upward. Usage rising with no change in how many people live in the home.
  • ✓Discolored water after a pressure drop. Rust-tinged water can mean the old steel line is shedding scale. We do not diagnose water-quality health risks, and the water utility is the place to ask.

Low water pressure: line, valve or regulator?

Pressure that is weak at every fixture, instead of just one, points toward the supply side. A partly closed main valve, a failing pressure regulator, a corroded service line and a leak all produce the same weak flow. A plumber measures with a gauge on a hose bib before guessing.

High pressure matters too. The California Plumbing Code calls for a regulator where static pressure is above 80 psi, and a utility such as San Diego Public Utilities suggests setting a new regulator between 45 and 75 psi. A failed regulator that lets pressure climb can wear on fixtures and joints, while one that sticks closed starves the house.

The pressure regulator on the line

The pressure regulator, also called a PRV, is a bell-shaped valve near where the line enters the house. It reduces street pressure to a level the plumbing can take. Symptoms of a failing one are banging pipes, fluctuating pressure or a gauge reading that creeps up overnight.

Replacing a PRV is a short job when the valve is accessible. If an expansion tank is needed downstream, the code ties that to the regulator as well. Ask your plumber to show the gauge reading before and after.

Trenching or trenchless: how the new line goes in

Replacing a long service line can be done two ways. Which one fits depends on what is above the pipe.

MethodHow it worksBest whenWatch out for
Open trenchA trench is dug along the pipe, the old line is removed and new pipe is laidShort runs in open yard, or where the pipe is shallow and easy to reachLawn, landscaping and driveway edges are disturbed
Trenchless (pipe bursting and similar)Pits are dug at each end and the new pipe is pulled through or the old pipe is broken apart in placeLong runs under driveways, patios or mature plantingsNeeds access pits and suitable soil and pipe
Spot repairOne section of pipe is dug out and replacedA single break in a healthy lineMore breaks may appear later in aged pipe

NASSCO, the trenchless industry association, says these methods can repair or replace a pipe without digging it up. Whether a water line qualifies depends on depth, access and the pipe you have now.

Repair or replace the line?

A single break in an otherwise sound pipe is a candidate for a spot repair. If the line is galvanized steel, one reference says it typically lasts 40 to 60 years, so a corroded line that has failed once is likely to fail again nearby. In that case a full replacement saves repeat digging.

If the line split suddenly and water is spraying, the work resembles burst pipe repair, just underground. Ask the plumber to show you the cut section of pipe. Heavy scale, pitting or a thinned wall tells you more than any rule of thumb. Replacement may also need a permit or an inspection, which you should confirm with the City of Wasco before the trench opens.

Before anyone digs: locating buried utilities

Digging into a yard without marking utilities can hit gas, electric or communication lines.

  1. Call 811The national call-before-you-dig number routes you to the locate service for your area. Request marks for the planned dig zone.
  2. Wait for the marksUtilities mark the lines they own. Private lines, like a yard light feed or an irrigation main, may not be marked, so tell the plumber what you know.
  3. Stay in the marked zoneHand-dig near marked lines, and keep clear of the paint marks.
  4. Treat gas smells as a stop signSoCalGas serves Wasco. If you smell gas at any point, leave the area and call SoCalGas or 911 from a safe distance.
